# CivicSentra Phase 0 — Build Specification

**Version 1.0 · July 2026**
Reference implementation of Phase 0 (Shadow Mode) from *CivicSentra: A
Sustainable AI-Driven Model for Securing Transactions Across E-Commerce,
Banking, and Public Welfare Systems.*

---

## 1. Purpose

The white paper makes four claims that had no evidence behind them. This build
exists to test all four and report what actually happens. Three were tested;
one was not.

| # | Claim | Paper § | How we test it | Status |
|---|-------|---------|----------------|--------|
| C1 | Scoring completes in under 30 ms | §4.2 | Per-transaction latency benchmark, p50/p95/p99, streaming not batched | **Tested — not supported.** p50 636.79 ms, p95 1044.21 ms, p99 1097.04 ms; 0% of sampled transactions under 30 ms. Three runs over four days agree to 0.69% at the median |
| C2 | Fraud reduced 60–80% | §4.4 | Detection rate at operationally realistic false-positive rates | **Tested — partially supported.** 72.94% recall at the configured operating point, inside the claimed band, but at 17.12% precision; see §4.4 on the review-volume cost |
| C3 | Federated learning beats siloed models without sharing raw data | §4.2, §7 | Three-partition federated averaging vs. local-only baselines | **NOT TESTED.** `federated.py` was cut from this sprint and is not built. No evidence is offered for C3 either way |
| C4 | Savings vastly exceed cost ("spend 1, save 99") | §4.4, §6 | Dollar-weighted cost model producing the *actual* ratio | **Tested — partially supported.** See below |

**C1 needs stating plainly:** the measured latency is roughly 21× the claimed
bound at the median. The dominant cause is architectural, not language choice:
`features.py` recomputes every feature from full history on each call rather
than maintaining incremental state, and latency correlates with history size at
r=0.997–0.999 across three runs. A compiled reimplementation would not by
itself close this gap.

**C4 needs stating plainly:** Measured: 3.15:1 to 4.52:1 across the
review-cost range. The paper's §4.4 figure of 20–80x is not supported; the §6
figure of 4–5x is. The "spend 1, save 99" formulation appears nowhere in the
paper's own arithmetic and is withdrawn. The measured ratio remains above
break-even throughout the cost range. The dominant constraint is review
volume, driven by SPEND_BASELINE_HIGH_DRAW on ordinary legitimate
transactions — see the README roadmap for intended remediation.

## 2. Scope

> **Scope note — what was actually built (2026-07-27).** This document was
> written as a build specification before the build. It describes a
> three-rail system evaluated on synthetic *and* public benchmark data. That
> is not what exists.
>
> **What exists is the EBT rail only, on synthetic data only.** No public
> benchmark dataset was downloaded or used. The card rail (§3.4) and CNP rail
> (§3.5) were not built. `federated.py` (§4.6) and `report.py` (§4.7) were not
> built. Graph-based ring detection (§4.2) was not built.
>
> Sections below are left as originally written, because they record the
> design reasoning and the sourcing decisions behind what *was* built. Where
> a section describes something unbuilt, it now carries a marker saying so.
> Read this document as a specification with a delivery record attached, not
> as a description of a finished system.
>
> **The consequence for interpretation, stated once here:** because every
> fraud pattern in the evaluation was specified and injected by the author,
> a reviewer may reasonably object that the system detects only the patterns
> it was told to look for. The card rail existed in this design specifically
> to retire that objection. It was not built, so **the objection stands.**

**In scope.** A shadow-mode risk scoring engine operating over three transaction
rails, evaluated for detection performance, cost-effectiveness, latency, and
fairness, on synthetic and public benchmark data.

**Out of scope.** Production deployment. Real payment network integration. Real
cardholder or beneficiary data. Any claim that this constitutes a pilot.

**This distinction must appear in the README, the report, and §9.5 of the paper.**
Overstating what this is would be worse than not building it.

## 3. Data strategy

Three rails. One we create because it cannot be obtained; two are public.

### 3.1 EBT rail — synthetic, generated by us (PRIORITY)

There is no public dataset of EBT/SNAP fraud transactions. Privacy law and
program rules make one unlikely to ever exist. This is precisely why §5.3 of the
paper is the weakest section — and why building a credible synthetic benchmark
is a genuine contribution rather than a workaround.

**Generator requirements:**

- Configurable population of cardholders, each with a home state, a benefit
  issuance day (states stagger issuance across the month by case number), a
  monthly benefit amount, a home-area store cluster, and a `locality_class`
  (urban / suburban / rural — decided 2026-07-23, needed for the fairness
  audit in §4.7; see note there for why)
- Legitimate behaviour: purchases at a small set of nearby stores, spending
  drawn down across the benefit month, realistic amount distributions, realistic
  time-of-day patterns
- Terminal population with geographic coordinates, store size class, and a small
  subset designated as compromised
- Labelled fraud episodes injected according to the patterns in §3.2
- Labelled N1 (technical failure) and N2 (legitimate third-party use) episodes
  per §3.3, each `is_fraud = False`

**Ground truth:** every row carries a boolean `is_fraud` and, where applicable, a
`fraud_pattern` label (a P-code, N1, or N2) so we can report detection and
false-flag rates broken down by category. That breakdown is far more useful
than a single aggregate number — it tells a state agency which attacks the
system catches, which it misses, and which legitimate behaviour it wrongly
flags.

### 3.2 EBT fraud patterns to encode

**Sourcing note (decided 2026-07-24).** The author has no direct casework on
card or EBT skimming. P1–P8 below are therefore sourced from the public
record, not from personal experience, and each parameter is either cited or
explicitly marked as an unsupported assumption. The author's direct experience
instead grounds N1, N2 (§3.3) and the feature-family design in §4.2 — that
division must be stated plainly in README.md and `EXPLAIN_ebt_generator.md` so
every number in the generator can be traced to the correct kind of source.

| Pattern | Description | Public-record status (2026-07-24) |
|---|---|---|
| P1 · Skimmer harvest | Victim cards transact normally at a compromised terminal; card and PIN captured | Confirmed as a method (GAO-25-107964 Table 1, "card skimming"). No published figure for compromise window or victims-per-terminal — **use a labelled assumption**. |
| P2 · Test transaction | Small-value transaction on the cloned card before the drain | **Not documented anywhere in the public record found.** Card-fraud literature describes this pattern generally; no EBT-specific figure exists — **labelled assumption only**. |
| P3 · Balance probe | Balance inquiry immediately preceding the drain | Confirmed and named: FNS's "algorithmic attacks" — bots exploit online balance-inquiry tools "to identify valid PINs" and cards with positive balances (GAO-25-107964 p.2, p.6). FNS restricted online balance inquiries to 2/session in Jan 2025 specifically because of this. No frequency/volume figure — **assumption for magnitude, not existence**. |
| P4 · Issuance-day drain | Cloned card empties the balance within hours of monthly issuance | Confirmed structurally: "card cloning" is defined as using cloned cards to steal benefits "after the state SNAP agency reloads benefits" (GAO-25-107964 p.2). No timing figure — **labelled assumption for "how fast"**. |
| P5 · Out-of-state use | Drain occurs far from the cardholder's home area | Confirmed: FNS states thieves "largely use stolen SNAP account information to make purchases in a different state from the recipient or through online platforms" (GAO-25-107964 p.7, out-of-state/online blocking pilot). No distance figure — **labelled assumption**. |
| P6 · Terminal clustering | Many cloned cards drained through a small number of terminals | One anecdote, not a distribution: a state SNAP official reported $60,000 stolen in one morning via a hijacked FNS retailer number (GAO-25-107964 p.14). Cards-per-terminal count — **labelled assumption**. |
| P7 · Off-hours activity | Transactions at times inconsistent with the cardholder's history | **Not documented in the public record found.** Predictive value unconfirmed either way — **labelled assumption, and evaluate.py should report whether this feature actually adds detection power, per the original spec question**. |
| P8 · PIN guessing on cloned cards | Repeated PIN attempts against a stolen card number | Confirmed as a named vector: same "algorithmic attacks" bots test/identify valid PINs (GAO-25-107964 p.2); USDA OIG recommended forced periodic PIN changes; FNS recommends blocking common PINs like "1234" (p.6, p.8). No attempt-count or lockout figure — **labelled assumption**. |

Primary source: U.S. GAO, *Nutrition Assistance: USDA Should Comprehensively
Assess Benefit Theft Prevention Measures States Are Implementing*,
GAO-25-107964 (Sept. 25, 2025). Also worth noting as a citable finding in its
own right: GAO reports that FNS itself does not collect data on *where or how*
benefits were stolen, because most recipients don't know (p.3) — the public
record's silence on operational parameters is structural, not an oversight on
our part.

### 3.3 Legitimate-but-anomalous categories — N1 and N2

Two categories that must be generated and labelled `is_fraud = False`, because
they are the cases a naive detector confuses with fraud. Both are sourced from
the author's direct dispute-handling experience, not the public record.

**N1 · Technical failure, anomalous but legitimate (decided 2026-07-24).**
Grounded in real casework from the author's time in banking IT security:
cardholders used other banks' ATMs (off-us), the transaction failed on a
technical fault in interbank routing, the customer never received cash, but
the amount was debited anyway.

- *Two dispute types are behaviourally opposite underneath, even though they
  look identical to the customer:* fraud = money dispensed, cardholder not
  present; technical failure = cardholder present, no money dispensed, still
  debited.
- *Auto-reversal filters the easy cases out before they ever reach a fraud
  team.* Some failures reverse automatically and the customer never disputes
  them. The generator must produce a larger pool of technical failures with a
  self-resolving fraction that never enters the labelled dataset — only the
  residue that failed to auto-reverse should appear as N1 rows. This fraction
  is not something the author measured; treat it as a configurable, labelled
  assumption, not a target to hit.
- *How the author found the pattern:* disputes arrived individually and
  looked unrelated; scanning logs revealed a shared cause. This is the
  real-world basis for treating "temporal-neighbour" and graph-style
  clustering as detection mechanisms (§4.2), not just a generation detail.
- *Detection lag is rail-dependent and must be a configurable parameter, not a
  constant.* Card rail: same-day to next-day, because SMS transaction alerts
  let the customer notice immediately and call the same day (night-time
  failures are the exception — some customers wait for the branch to open).
  EBT rail: days to weeks, because SNAP recipients typically have no
  transaction alerting and discover theft/failure when the card declines at
  the till. **This gap is a notification-infrastructure difference between
  the rails, not a technical one, and belongs in the report as a finding, not
  just a modelling detail.**
- EBT-specific mechanism: store-and-forward under 7 CFR 274.12(m) — a
  retailer may hold transactions during an outage and forward them within 24
  hours; if that window crosses a new issuance period, the retailer may draw
  against the full new balance. Behaviourally identical to P4 (issuance-day
  drain).
- `evaluate.py` reports the N1 false-flag rate as a measured output. It is not
  an input target and must not be estimated in advance.

**N2 · Legitimate third-party use (decided 2026-07-24).** Grounded in real
casework: a cardholder insisted they had made no transaction; a family member
had used the card with their knowledge. Not fraud. This matters more on the EBT
rail than in banking, because household members routinely use SNAP benefits
cards and "authorised representative" is a formal role in the program — "the
named cardholder didn't personally transact" does not imply fraud.

- Generate as same-household transactions with a pattern slightly different
  from the primary user's: a different store within the same home-area
  cluster, a different time of day, a different basket size.
- `evaluate.py` reports the N2 false-flag rate separately from N1, alongside
  the ordinary legitimate-transaction false-positive rate.

### 3.4 Card rail — public benchmark

> **NOT BUILT — deferred to v1.1.** No Kaggle dataset was downloaded and no
> card-rail evaluation exists. The objection this section was written to
> retire (see "Why this rail matters" below) therefore stands against the
> current results. This is the single most consequential gap in the build.

The Sparkov-derived *Credit Card Transactions Fraud Detection* dataset (Kaggle).
Chosen over the better-known ULB dataset because it retains cardholder and
merchant latitude/longitude, transaction amount, merchant category, and
timestamps — meaning the geo-velocity and spend-baseline features in §4.3 can be
computed directly rather than approximated. Requires a free Kaggle account.

**Why this rail matters:** it is fraud we did not invent. Without it, a reviewer
can reasonably say we detected only the patterns we ourselves injected. This
rail retires that objection, which is why it is worth the download friction.

## 4. Modules

### 4.1 `ebt_generator.py`

Produces `data/ebt_synthetic.csv` plus `data/ebt_data_dictionary.md`. Seeded and
reproducible. Fraud prevalence configurable, defaulting to a realistic low base
rate — fraud detection is an imbalanced-data problem and a 50/50 dataset would
make the whole evaluation meaningless.

### 4.2 `features.py`

The five feature families from §4.3 of the paper, plus two added from the
author's direct investigative experience on 2026-07-24 (home-location plausibility,
terminal temporal-neighbour analysis — see below).

**Geo-velocity.** Haversine distance between consecutive transactions on the same
card, divided by elapsed time, yielding implied travel speed. Flag physically
impossible values. Handle the first transaction per card, and simultaneous
timestamps, without dividing by zero.

*Breakpoint sourcing (decided 2026-07-24):* the author never used a speed
threshold operationally, relying on home-location plausibility instead
(below), not geo-velocity. The impossible-travel bound for this feature is
therefore derived from physics, not from casework, and must be cited as such:
commercial aircraft cruise around 900 km/h, so an implied speed above
roughly 1,000 km/h (allowing for ground time) is a physical impossibility
regardless of domain judgment.

**Home-location plausibility (new, decided 2026-07-24, the author's direct
experience).** Distinct from geo-velocity — geo-velocity is distance between
two consecutive transactions; this is whether a transaction is consistent
with where this person normally transacts. Grounded in a real case: a
customer's transaction location was compared against a broad zone around his
*registered home address*, not a whitelist of his previously-visited
locations — a whitelist would falsely flag every first visit to a new store.
For EBT this matters more than in banking: authorised retailers open, close,
and change, and people move.

- Zone radius scales with `locality_class` (urban/suburban/rural) — rural
  recipients travel further to reach an authorised retailer than urban ones,
  so a single fixed radius would flag rural households constantly for
  ordinary behaviour. Report the false-positive rate by `locality_class` for
  this feature specifically, to confirm the scaling actually worked (ties to
  the fairness audit, §4.7).
- Distance and hour-of-day are an **interaction, not two separate additive
  features** — the same distance from home should score higher at unusual
  hours than during normal shopping hours. A transaction far from home in the
  afternoon is unremarkable; the same distance at 2am is what prompted
  investigation in the real case this is drawn from.

**Terminal temporal-neighbour analysis (new, decided 2026-07-24, the
author's direct experience).** Distinct from device/terminal reputation below —
reputation is the slow historical fraud rate at a terminal over weeks; this
looks at what else happened at the *same terminal within minutes* of the
transaction being scored (the transaction immediately before and immediately
after, in both directions). Grounded in a real investigative method: a single
disputed transaction told the author very little, but the neighbouring
at the same terminal often did — this is how an EBT skimming cluster (many
cloned cards run through one terminal in a burst) would surface even when
each individual transaction looks acceptable on its own.

**No numeric threshold (e.g. "N disputes at one terminal") is attributed to
the author for this feature.** Any window size or count threshold must be derived
from the generated data's own structure or cited to a published source, and
labelled accordingly in `config.yaml`.

**Device and terminal reputation.** Rolling historical fraud rate per terminal.
Apply Bayesian shrinkage toward the global mean so that a terminal with two
transactions and one fraud does not score as 50% risk. Compute using only data
available *before* each transaction — no future information.

*Card rail mapping (decided 2026-07-23):* merchant substitutes for terminal.
Sparkov provides merchant identity and merchant lat/long, so the feature is
computable unchanged in mechanics. **Document this as a limitation, not a
detail:** a merchant is an aggregate of many devices; a terminal is one
physical device. Compromise clusters at a single terminal, but merchant-level
reputation smears that signal across every device the merchant operates.
Expect this feature — and the graph feature below, for the same reason — to
perform worse on the card rail, and report that openly rather than only on
the aggregate metric.

**Spend baseline deviation (revised 2026-07-24).** Not a generic z-score on
raw amount — EBT spending isn't open-ended like bank spending. A household
gets a fixed benefit on a fixed day and draws it down on a predictable curve:
a large shop soon after issuance, smaller trips after, tapering to near zero
by month end. "Normal amount" therefore depends almost entirely on where the
household is in the benefit cycle; amount alone is close to meaningless
without cycle position.

Build this feature as **percentage of remaining balance, combined with
days-since-issuance** — not a z-score on raw dollar amount. A $180
transaction on issuance day is ordinary; the same $180 on day 25 usually
isn't possible, because the money isn't there. This also sharpens the fraud
signal: a cloned card draining most of the remaining balance in one
transaction is a stronger indicator than "unusually large amount" alone.

*Sourcing:* this is structural reasoning about how the SNAP benefit cycle
works, not something the author observed in casework — label it as such in
`config.yaml`, distinct from the N1/N2 provenance below.

*Deliberate collision with N1:* a legitimate store-and-forward transaction
that crosses into a new issuance period (§3.3, N1) can also draw a high
percentage of the new balance — this feature will fire on genuine N1 cases as
well as real fraud (P4). Separating the two is exactly what `evaluate.py`
measures (N1 false-flag rate); this is not a bug to tune away.

Hour of day moves to the home-location-plausibility feature above, as an
interaction term rather than a separate baseline dimension. Merchant-category
novelty over an expanding window is unchanged.

**Graph-based ring detection.** Card↔terminal bipartite graph. Connected
components. Flag components where a small number of terminals connect an unusual
number of cards within a short window — the structural signature of a skimming
cluster. Complements terminal temporal-neighbour analysis above: the graph
feature finds ring structure across many cards over a longer window; neighbour
analysis catches a burst at a single terminal within minutes. Both trace back
to the same real investigative insight — the author found the original technical-
failure batch (§3.3, N1) by noticing that individually-unremarkable disputes
shared a cause once looked at together, not by any one transaction alone.

*Card rail mapping:* card↔merchant bipartite graph, merchant substituting for
terminal — same limitation as above applies, and applies more sharply here,
since ring structure is the thing merchant-level aggregation smears hardest.

**EBT policy rules.** Out-of-state without travel indication; drain velocity
relative to issuance day; the patterns from §3.2.

*Card rail mapping:* not applicable. Drop this feature family entirely for the
card rail — do not invent an equivalent. Geo-velocity and spend baseline
deviation port to the card rail unchanged.

> **Leakage warning.** Every feature must be computable from information
> available at the moment the transaction arrives. If a feature uses data from
> the future, the results are worthless and the mistake is invisible in the
> metrics. Include an explicit test for this.

### 4.3 `scorer.py`

Weighted combination of features into a 0–100 risk score. Two thresholds split
the range into **allow / step-up / block**. Every decision returns reason codes
naming the contributing factors.

Weights and thresholds live in `config.yaml`. Initial weights come from the
author's judgment, not from optimization — the paper describes an expert-informed system
with governed thresholds, and the implementation should match.

**Normalization (decided 2026-07-23).** Each feature maps to a bounded 0–1
sub-score via a monotonic transform whose breakpoints are set from domain
judgment. Weights across features sum to 1.0. Final score is the weighted sum
of sub-scores, times 100.

Example shape (breakpoint numbers to be confirmed in the Day 1 domain
session, not invented here): geo-velocity contributes 0 below ordinary travel
speed, ramps across an implausible band, saturates at 1 above commercial
flight speed.

Explicitly rejected, and why:

- *Percentile or rank transforms computed over the dataset.* Two problems.
  They are a leakage vector — the full distribution includes future
  transactions relative to any given scoring moment. And they turn every
  parameter into an artifact of this particular dataset rather than a domain
  judgment, which breaks the interpretability the whole design depends on.
- *Optimizing weights against the data.* On a synthetic dataset whose fraud
  we specified, that would be circular. It also contradicts the paper, which
  describes an expert-informed system with governed thresholds — the code
  must match the claim, not quietly become a different kind of system.

The test for every breakpoint: can it be explained in one sentence to a state
SNAP administrator? If not, it's wrong. All transforms and breakpoints live in
`config.yaml`, each with a comment stating its domain justification.

### 4.4 `evaluate.py`

- Precision, recall, **PR-AUC** (not ROC-AUC — ROC-AUC flatters classifiers on
  imbalanced data and would overstate performance)
- **Alerts per 1,000 transactions at fixed recall levels.** This is how review
  capacity is actually planned. Reporting it signals operational literacy.
- Detection rate **broken down by fraud pattern** (P1–P8)
- **Dollar cost matrix:** fraud value caught, fraud value missed, legitimate
  value wrongly blocked, plus review cost per alert. Produces the real
  savings-to-cost ratio for C4.
- Threshold sensitivity: how the above move as thresholds shift
- **False-flag rate on N1 (technical failure) and N2 (legitimate third-party
  use), reported separately from each other and from the ordinary
  legitimate-transaction false-positive rate (decided 2026-07-24, §3.3).**
  These are measured outputs, not targets — neither category's expected
  false-flag rate was estimated in advance, by design.

**Small-subgroup confidence intervals (decided 2026-07-24).** Any subgroup
metric computed on fewer than ~200 cases must be reported with its raw count
and a 95% confidence interval alongside the point estimate — not the point
estimate alone. This applies to every subgroup breakdown in this section and
in the fairness audit (§4.7), but the N1 crossing-issuance-boundary subgroup
is the specific case that prompted it: 48 rows in the primary dataset (§3.3).
State the wide interval plainly rather than let a small-n point estimate
read as more precise than it is.

**Review cost per alert (decided 2026-07-23; figure supplied 2026-07-24).**
This is a domain estimate, not a fact, so it must not be treated as one.
The author's range: **$6.40–$9.20, midpoint $7.50**, derived from US
fraud-analyst fully-loaded cost per alert (base salary $62k public-sector to
$89k private, ×1.3 fully-loaded multiplier, ÷1,800 productive hours/year,
÷~7 alerts/hour blended across ~85% quick triage and ~15% escalation),
calibrated against the author's dispute-handling experience in banking. It lives in `config.yaml` as a range
(`review_cost_per_alert_usd: {low, mid, high}`), and `evaluate.py` must run
the full C4 cost-ratio calculation across that entire range, not just at the
midpoint. If the C4 conclusion (whether savings meaningfully exceed cost, and
by how much) flips anywhere inside that range, that is the most important
finding in the report and belongs in the headline — not a footnote. A cost
ratio that rests on one point estimate is not evidence.

### 4.5 `benchmark_latency.py`

End-to-end per-transaction scoring time — feature computation included, one
transaction at a time. **Not vectorized batch processing.** Batch throughput
would produce an impressively small number that has nothing to do with the
authorization-time latency §4.2 claims, and using it would be a form of cheating.

Report p50, p95, p99, hardware specs, and this caveat: Python is not a
production authorization-path language, so these figures represent an upper
bound that a compiled implementation would improve on.

### 4.6 `federated.py`

> **NOT BUILT — deferred to v1.1.** This module does not exist. Claim C3 is
> consequently untested; §1's table records that. Nothing in the results
> supports or contradicts the paper's federated-learning claim.

Partition the data into three simulated states. Train a local logistic
regression per state. Average the coefficients (FedAvg). Compare, on a held-out
state, the federated model's detection against that state's local-only model.

The interesting case is deliberately asymmetric: give one state a fraud pattern
the others have not seen, and test whether the federated model transfers
knowledge of it. That directly tests the paper's claim that federation stops
fraud rings migrating between jurisdictions.

### 4.7 `report.py`

> **NOT BUILT — deferred to v1.1.** `outputs/PHASE0_REPORT.md` does not
> exist. The measured numbers live in `outputs/evaluate_metrics.md` and
> `outputs/benchmark_latency.md`, written directly by `evaluate.py` and
> `benchmark_latency.py`. The fairness audit below is therefore only
> partially delivered: `evaluate.py` reports the aggregate false-positive
> rate by `locality_class` (rural 1.32%, suburban 1.46%, urban 1.36%), but
> the **per-feature** breakdown this section asks for — specifically, whether
> home-location plausibility's locality-scaled zone radius actually removed
> the disparity it was designed to remove — was not built and remains an
> open question, not a settled one.

Generates `outputs/PHASE0_REPORT.md` with every metric above, charts, run
configuration, and timestamp. Fully reproducible from a single command.

Must also include the **fairness audit** §7 commits to: false-positive rate
broken out by segment (locality_class on the EBT rail, `city_pop`-derived band
on the card rail — Sparkov carries `city_pop` natively; benefit amount band;
rail). If a disparity appears, report it — an honest fairness finding is more
valuable than a clean one, because it demonstrates the audit mechanism
actually works. Every segment breakdown here is subject to the small-subgroup
confidence-interval rule in §4.4 — a segment with under ~200 cases gets its
count and a 95% CI stated alongside the rate, not the rate alone.

*Note (decided 2026-07-23):* `locality_class` exists specifically because
rural SNAP recipients legitimately travel further to reach authorized
retailers than urban recipients do, so a naive geo-velocity feature will flag
them at a higher rate for normal behavior. The generator must let this
disparity appear if it's real — it must not be tuned to be absent. If
geo-velocity false-positive rates differ by locality_class, that is the
fairness audit working, not a bug to fix quietly.

*Extension (decided 2026-07-24):* the home-location-plausibility feature
(§4.2) explicitly scales its zone radius by `locality_class` to *prevent*
this disparity. Report the false-positive rate by `locality_class` for that
feature too, specifically to check whether the scaling worked — a
still-disparate result there would mean the adjustment failed, not that the
feature should be quietly retuned until the disparity disappears.
