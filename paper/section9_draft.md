9.2 Two categories absent from the fraud literature

Two classes of transaction in this dataset are entirely legitimate yet carry the behavioural signature of fraud. Neither appears in the published fraud-detection literature. Both come from disputed-transaction investigation: I encountered them while handling card disputes in banking IT security, and they shape both the feature design and the evaluation reported in this section. This is a different provenance from the fraud patterns P1–P8, which are drawn from GAO-25-107964, including that report's account of FNS and USDA OIG findings and of state agency experience, rather than from personal observation.

N1 — technical failure. In the course of investigating card disputes I identified a set of transactions in which our cardholders had used other banks' ATMs, the transaction had failed on an interbank routing fault, no cash was dispensed, and the account was debited regardless. These did not arrive as a batch. They presented individually, appearing unrelated, and the common cause emerged only from scanning the transaction logs.

What makes this category consequential is that two opposite events are indistinguishable from the complainant's side. In the fraud case, cash is dispensed and the cardholder was not present. In the technical failure, the cardholder was present and cash was not dispensed. The customer's report is the same in both: money left the account and I did not receive it.

A further effect narrows what a detector ever observes. Some routing failures reverse automatically, and the customer is reimbursed without a dispute being raised. The technical failures that reach a fraud team are therefore those where automatic reversal did not occur. The easy cases have already been filtered out; the observable population is the residual hard cases by construction.

The EBT analogue is established by regulation rather than by analogy. Under 7 CFR § 274.12(m) a retailer may store transactions when the EBT central processing system is unreachable and forward them once connectivity is restored, once, within 24 hours. Where that window crosses into a new benefit issuance period, the retailer may draw against the full newly available balance. A legitimate store-and-forward transaction can therefore arrive delayed, out of sequence, and drawing a substantial proportion of a freshly issued balance — which is the same behavioural profile as a cloned card drained on issuance day (pattern P4).

The dataset contains 339 N1 transactions, of which 48 are store-and-forward cases crossing an issuance boundary, drawing a mean 68.4% of monthly benefit (median 71.1%, range 42.6–88.3%). These 48 are the sharpest test in the evaluation: they are legitimate, they are regulation-permitted, and they are behaviourally near-identical to the pattern the system is designed to catch.

N2 — legitimate third-party use. A second dispute type recurred: a cardholder reported a transaction he had not made, received the corresponding notification, and was telling the truth — a family member had used the card. No fraud had occurred.

This carries greater weight in benefits delivery than in retail banking. Benefit cards are routinely used by household members, and authorised representatives are a formal feature of programme administration. The inference that the named cardholder did not personally transact therefore does not support the conclusion that the transaction was fraudulent. The dataset contains 1,686 N2 transactions.

Why these categories are necessary. Detection systems are conventionally evaluated on fraud caught and fraud missed. Neither figure indicates how often a system flags transactions that were never fraudulent. That omission has an operational cost: alerts of this kind cannot be resolved by investigation, because there is nothing fraudulent to find. Including N1 and N2 as labelled, separately-reported populations makes that cost measurable rather than invisible.

The measured outcome, reported in full in 9.3, does not follow the intuition. N1 carries a comparatively high false-flag rate of 16.22% (55 of 339) and N2 a low rate of 0.95%, but the two together account for only 3.68% of all false positives. The remaining 96.32% are ordinary legitimate transactions flagging at 1.38% across a population approximately 397 times larger. A small rate on a very large base dominates a high rate on a small one — a result that would have been unavailable without the categories being labelled and counted separately.
